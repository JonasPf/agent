Jobs

A job is a rule attached to a conversation. It is not a session and holds no history of its own.

When the user says "tell me when the deploy finishes", the agent creates a job. The job runs inside the conversation where it was created, so its results appear where the request was made and its context is already present without being restated.

Shape

A job wakes on a schedule and runs a prompt. Everything else is optional and independent.

FieldMeaning
session_idThe conversation this job runs in. Required.
scheduleWhen it wakes: an interval (30m), a cron expression (0 9 * * *), or a single absolute time. Required.
promptWhat the agent is asked when it acts. Required.
checkOptional. A shell command run before the prompt; exit status zero means act, anything else means skip this wake. Absent means always act.
after_actingWhat becomes of the job once it has acted: stop or continue. Defaults to continue.
statusDerived from what has happened, not set: scheduled until the job has nothing left to do, then done. A done job is kept, with its log.
There are no kinds. A job is not classified, and nothing branches on what sort of job it is. Every difference between the cases below is a difference in these values, which is why none of them needs an exemption from a rule meant for another.

One path. A check, if there is one, decides whether this wake acts, and after_acting decides whether the job survives it. Nothing consults what sort of job it is, because it is not any sort. Every wake writes a line to the job's log, whichever way it went.

What the table decides

Nothing is exempt from anything. The three exemptions the old design needed — a reminder skipping the cost floor, skipping reason_no_check, and skipping expiry — all disappear, because each was an exemption granted to a category, and there are no categories. A single absolute time has no interval for a floor to compare against, no condition to explain, and no default deadline to escape.

Ending is a field, not a consequence. "Tell me when the deploy finishes" is the case that used to need two mechanisms: a job kind to make the check cheap, and on_condition_met to stop it afterwards. It is now after_acting: stop — one field, saying exactly what it means at the moment it means it.

Cost is shown, not enforced. A check runs a shell command and costs nothing; a job with no check runs a turn every time it wakes. Nothing refuses a schedule on the strength of that — a floor of fifteen minutes was tried and removed (ADR-029) — so every job carries an estimate instead: how often it wakes, what one wake sends, what that is a day, and what it costs where the model's price is known.

The estimate is computed on the way out and never stored, because the thing it prices grows: a wake re-sends the system prompt and the conversation as it stands, so today's figure is a floor that rises until the session compacts. A gated job estimates nothing per day — its wakes are a shell command — and reports what the wake that does fire will cost. A schedule that fires once is priced once. Where the model catalogue cannot be reached the tokens are still shown; a money figure is never invented.

A cron schedule has no single gap between firings — */20 9-16 * * 1-5 waits twenty minutes five times and then sixty-four hours — so its cadence is counted over a week from the moment it is asked and averaged into a day. The count covers one whole week whatever minute the question arrives on, including the minute the schedule itself fires on.

Why nothing expires. Jobs once expired 24 hours after creation, which quietly destroyed any reminder set further out than a day. A until field replaced it, and then that went too (ADR-028): a deadline is a rule that deletes the operator's work on a timer, and the run log makes it unnecessary. A job watching for something that never happens waits indefinitely — as a visible row, with a log saying how many times it has looked. Ending it is the operator's call, and it takes one tap.

Acceptance criteria

  • Every row above is expressible with these fields alone, and behaves as the row says.
  • A reminder scheduled arbitrarily far ahead fires, and is never deleted before its time.
  • A job with after_acting of stop is marked done after the first wake on which it acts, and not before, and never wakes again.
  • Any cadence is accepted. "Remind me every ten minutes" creates one job that wakes every ten minutes.
  • Creating a job answers with what it will cost: wakes a day, tokens a turn, tokens a day, and money where the price is known.
  • A gated job estimates no daily cost, and says what a wake that fires costs.
  • A job that wakes once is priced once rather than per day.
  • A cron schedule is priced by its firings over a week, and the figure is the same whether or not the moment it is asked is itself a firing minute.
  • An unreachable model catalogue costs the money figures and nothing else.
  • The estimate is never stored on the job.
  • No job carries or derives a kind, and no behaviour depends on one.

What a check may do

A check is a question asked over and over, so it has to answer the moment it is asked. It runs with a sixty second timeout and is killed at it. A check that sleeps, polls, or waits for anything is a mistake in the job, not a condition that happens to be false.

So the two outcomes are kept apart. A non-zero exit is an answer: the condition does not hold, the tick costs nothing, and the job waits for the next one. A check that times out or cannot be run at all has decided nothing, and is a job failure — it backs off, and after three it becomes a dead letter. A check that never decides anything must be visible, not quiet.

Killing the shell is not enough. A check that spawns a child leaves that child holding the output pipe, and reading the check's output waits for every writer to close it. The timeout has to bound the wait for output too, or a check that spawns a sleeping child outlives the deadline meant to stop it.

Acceptance criteria

  • A check is killed at sixty seconds, including one that has spawned a child holding its output pipe.
  • A check that times out, or that cannot be run, is a job failure and not a condition that is unmet.
  • A check that exits non-zero is a condition that is unmet, and costs no model tokens.

A job runs on its session's model, in its session's context, like any other turn. Nothing about a job is configured separately — no model of its own, no context of its own — so the cost of a wake that acts is proportional to the conversation it lives in, which the compaction threshold already bounds.

The agent is instructed to reach for a check first, because a check makes a wake free and a missing one makes every wake a turn.

Acceptance criteria

  • A wake whose check exits non-zero consumes no model tokens.
  • Every wake writes a transcript entry, whether or not it acted.
  • A job whose session is mid-turn defers to the next tick rather than queueing repeats.
  • The agent attempts to express every condition as a check before creating a job without one.
  • A message produced by a job is attributed to that job in the interface.

Acting

A wake acts when its check exits zero, or when it has no check. Acting runs the prompt as a turn in the session, and the whole conversation it produces is written to the transcript, attributed to the job.

There is no signal by which the model can mark a wake as significant. It ends a job the way anything else ends one: by deleting it with the schedule tool, an ordinary visible call. A job set to after_acting: stop deletes itself after the first wake that acts.

A wake reaches the model marked as one, naming the job, saying how late it ran, and saying the operator is not present. Without that marker a wake is indistinguishable from the operator typing the same words, and the model answers it as if someone were reading — asking a question, or waiting for one. The marker says there is no one to answer: say what the wake is for, and leave it in the transcript, which is where the operator will read it.

Acceptance criteria

  • A wake reaches the model marked as a wake, naming its job and saying the operator is not present.
  • A message the operator typed carries no such marker.

Ending

A job ends in one of three ways: it acts and stops, its schedule runs out of wakes, or the operator deletes it. The first two leave the job in place, marked done, because its log is the record of what it did and deleting the row would delete the answer. Only the operator deletes a job, and nothing else ever does.

stateDiagram-v2 [*] --> Scheduled Scheduled --> Scheduled: woke, check said no Scheduled --> Acted: woke and acted Acted --> Done: after_acting = stop Acted --> Scheduled: after_acting = continue Scheduled --> Done: no further wake in the schedule Scheduled --> Scheduled: failed, logged, backed off Done --> [*]: deleted by the operator Scheduled --> [*]: deleted by the operator

A reminder ends by the first route: it wakes once, acts, and is marked done. It stays in the list with its log, so "did that reminder actually fire?" is answered where the job is, not by reading the conversation it fired into.

Kept long enough, the done ones bury the scheduled ones: a session that reminds every ten minutes leaves a row behind every ten minutes. So the operator can clear the finished jobs of a list in one action, taking their logs with them. It reaches nothing else. The state to delete is named by the caller rather than assumed, and only done is accepted, so nothing — a mistyped URL included — is one keystroke away from deleting every schedule the agent holds. Clearing is still only ever the operator's: the agent has no bulk delete.

Acceptance criteria

  • Every finished job in a list is deleted in one action, and its run log goes with it.
  • A job that is still scheduled is never deleted by that action.
  • Clearing from one conversation's jobs leaves every other conversation's alone.
  • A bulk delete that does not name done as the state it deletes is refused, and deletes nothing.
  • Clearing when nothing is finished succeeds and deletes nothing.
  • The interface offers the clear only while there are finished jobs, says how many it will delete, and asks before deleting them.

The run log

Every wake a job has had is recorded on the job: when it was due, when it ran, what came of it, and what was said. That is the answer to "has this been running?", and it is kept where the question is asked. A job outlives any one turn of the conversation that created it, and outlives every compaction of it, so its history cannot live only in what the transcript still sends.

FieldMeaning
atWhen the wake ran.
due_atWhen it was scheduled for. The two differ when the host slept, the session was busy, or the run was retried.
outcomefired, skipped, or failed.
messageWhat the agent said, why the check said no, or what went wrong.

This replaced dead letters (ADR-028). A dead letter was a copy of a job made at the moment the system deleted it, which existed because deleting the job destroyed the evidence. Nothing deletes a job now, so nothing has to be preserved from it.

Acceptance criteria

  • Every wake writes one line to the job's log, whether it acted, skipped, or failed.
  • A line that acted carries what the agent said; one that failed carries the failure.
  • A line records when the wake ran and when it was due, so a late run is visible as one.
  • The log belongs to the job and survives every compaction of the session it fires into.
  • Deleting a job deletes its log; nothing else does.
  • A job's log travels with it in a session export.

Failure handling

FailureResponse
Transient — timeout, 5xx, rate limitRetried inside the model call. If the wake still fails: a failed line in the log, exponential backoff, and the job keeps its place.
Credit exhausted, authentication, model withdrawnNo retry. Trip the circuit breaker. The job keeps its place.
Tool error inside a runNot a job failure. The result is returned to the model, which decides what to do.
Check command exits non-zeroThe condition is not met. Not a failure.

Circuit breaker

An exhausted account fails every job at once. Without a breaker, one billing lapse fills every job's log with the same failure and spends the retry budget on each.

Acceptance criteria

  • Failures across three or more distinct jobs within one tick window pause the scheduler.
  • A paused scheduler shows one banner across every screen, naming the cause.
  • A job failing while the breaker is open waits for the probe rather than counting the outage against itself.
  • While the breaker is open, one held job is retried every five minutes as a probe.
  • A successful probe closes the breaker, resumes the schedule, and says so.
  • The breaker state is visible in the interface.

Management

The agent creates, lists, edits, and deletes jobs through a scheduler tool, so it can clean up after itself when the user changes their mind. The same operations are available in the interface.

Acceptance criteria

  • The interface shows a job's own fields — schedule, check, after_acting, prompt — its status, its next wake, and its log, with no derived label.
  • A job can be edited or deleted from the interface without involving the agent.
  • Deleting a session deletes its jobs, and each job takes its log with it.
  • A job stays on the session it was created in, for that session's whole life. Nothing moves it.
  • A fork does not copy jobs; the origin keeps them and keeps running them.
